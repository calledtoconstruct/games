# Missing-number equations for eye gaze and dwell

One browser game can cover Tiny Tots through Rush. The screen is always a quantity question or a three-term equation with one blank, answered by dwelling on a few large cards. Mouse, touch, and gaze select the same cards. Nothing is typed, dragged, traced, timed, or scored. What changes by age is the number range, the operation, which of the three positions may be blank, how many cards are offered, and how many challenges cap the sitting.

Standards below are United States Common Core end-of-year targets, not a description of every child. The playable range for a band should start at the easy end of that target. Gaze guidance below is written for children who use looking as access, including children with significant physical disability. This game also has mouse and touch, so a gaze miss must never be the only way forward.

## Evidence

### Ages 2–3 are “how many,” not equations

The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide [Teaching Math to Young Children](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/practiceguide/wwc_empg_summary_020714.pdf) (summary of Frye and colleagues; the companion [By the Numbers](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/practiceguide/wwc_empg_numbers_020714.pdf) overview says the recommendations are for teaching math to 3- to 5-year-olds, while the summary itself says preschool, prekindergarten, and kindergarten) rates as moderate the advice to teach number along a progression. First, children recognize and label collections of one to three without counting. Next they count one-to-one, using the last number as the total. Then they compare, attach numerals, and only after that solve basic adding and subtracting, moving to slightly larger numbers as they become adept. Recommendation 4 in the same summary says to keep informal language (“more,” “all together,” “take away”) until the idea is solid, and only then attach `+`, `−`, and `=`.

The NAEYC and NCTM joint position statement [Early Childhood Mathematics: Promoting Good Beginnings](https://www.naeyc.org/positionstatements/mathematics) (copyright 2002, updated 2010) charts two points on the path from age 3 to about age 6. Around age 3, many children count a collection of one to four, begin to treat the last word as “how many,” quickly label sets of one to three, and add or subtract nonverbally when the numbers are very small (one ball, then another, so the box has two). Around age 6, the same chart shows counting-on addition and subtraction whose totals do not go beyond 10.

For toddlers specifically, the Fall 2022 NAEYC article [Nurturing Early Math Play and Discovery](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/yc/fall2022/nurturing-early-math-play) describes asking children to collect one, two, or three objects, and introducing addition as “one more” while a tower is being built. A wrong count is not treated as failure: the adult counts again with the child and points once per object. The same article cites a longitudinal finding (Eason and Levine, as summarized there) that math words heard between 14 and 30 months predicted number understanding at 46 months. That is an association reported second-hand in this article, not a result re-checked here.

Established for this game: a real Tiny Tots round is matching or producing a set of 1–3, or choosing “one more,” with objects or pictures. A written `X + Y = Z` at this age skips the WWC progression.

### Ages 4–5 stay inside 5; ages 6–8 open all three blanks inside 20

[Kindergarten Operations and Algebraic Thinking](https://thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/K/OA/) expects addition and subtraction within 10 using objects or drawings, decompositions of numbers up to 10 (the standard’s own example is `5 = 2 + 3`), the partner that makes 10, and fluent addition and subtraction within 5. [Kindergarten Counting and Cardinality](https://www.thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/K/CC/) expects “how many” for up to 20 arranged objects or 10 scattered ones, and comparison of groups up to 10. Counting to 100 is a counting goal, not a reason to put 100 on a choice card.

[Playing Around with Number Composition](https://www.naeyc.org/resources/pubs/tyc/spring2022/number-composition) (Lange, Mano, Lech, and Nayfeld, NAEYC *Teaching Young Children*, Spring 2022) tells preschool teachers to start composition with numbers 1 through 5, using identical objects, and to move to larger numbers later. Their classroom example composes 6, and they note that fluent parts of a number are what later let a child find a missing cup when setting a table. They also list the supports that should already be underway: counting aloud to 10, one-to-one counting, cardinality, and magnitude.

[Grade 1 Operations and Algebraic Thinking](https://thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/1/OA/) is the first standard that requires the unknown in any position within 20, with fluency inside 10. The equation examples in 1.OA.D.8 are `8 + ? = 11`, `5 = _ − 3`, and `6 + 6 = _`. Subtraction is explicitly an unknown-addend problem (`10 − 8` means “what makes 10 with 8”). [Grade 2](https://thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/2/OA/) asks for fluent addition and subtraction within 20 and memory of all sums of two one-digit numbers, and for one- and two-step problems within 100. It also starts equal groups: odd and even up to 20, and arrays of up to 5 rows by 5 columns written as a sum of equal addends, not yet as multiplication facts.

The within-100 grade 2 expectation is for drawn word problems. It is a poor fit for four large cards unless the missing value is still chosen from a tiny set. That limit is inferred from the access constraint, not stated by the standard.

### Multiplication belongs at about 9, after equal groups make sense

[Grade 3 Operations and Algebraic Thinking](https://thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/3/OA/) introduces multiplication and division within 100, including an unknown in any of the three positions (`8 × ? = 48`, `5 = _ ÷ 3`, `6 × 6 = ?`). By the end of grade 3, students are expected to know from memory the products of two one-digit numbers. Division is taught as an unknown factor. The parent [Operations and Algebraic Thinking](https://thecorestandards.org/Math/Content/OA/) page continues, in grade 4, to factors and multiples through 100 and to multistep problems, and in grade 5 to expressions with parentheses. Those later forms are not three-term one-blank facts.

Zack Hill, writing for Illustrative Mathematics on [25 January 2021](https://illustrativemathematics.blog/2021/01/25/by-the-end-of-grade-3-developing-fluency-with-multiplication/), says the curriculum he led deliberately did not ask children for a product until they had several lessons on equal groups. Fluency was spread across the year. One center, Rectangle Rumble, grows in three stages: factors of 1, 2, 5, and 10; then factors 1–5; then factors 1–10. That is a claimed curriculum design tied to standard 3.OA.C.7, not an experimental result.

A child of about 9 is at the end of that grade 3 expectation or just past it. Multiplication and division of one-digit factors are appropriate then. They are not appropriate as the main task at 6–8, where grade 2 only lays the array and the repeated sum.

### The blank should move from the result to a part, then to the start

[Donna Boucher, 28 August 2024](https://www.mathcoachscorner.com/2024/08/solving-problems-with-the-unknown-in-any-position/), describes the classroom habit of staying on result unknown (`3 + 2 = □`) and says children also need part unknown (`3 + □ = 5`). She says kindergarten work on combinations through 10 should start as result unknown with counters, and that part-unknown cards come only after a lot of that practice, not on the same day for every child. She warns against naming a missing-part story “a subtraction problem,” because counting up is a valid way to find the hidden part.

[Jillian Starr, 5 November 2023](https://jillianstarrteaching.com/missing-addends/), says missing addends are hard because they require the equal sign, the meaning of addition, the link to subtraction, and counting on. She reports a common error when the equal sign is still “the answer comes next”: children put the two visible numbers on one side and write their sum in the blank (her example: the 5 and the 2 end up together, and the child writes 7).

A district extract of the grade 1 problem-type table, [Types of Problems](https://cdnsm5-ss16.sharpschool.com/UserFiles/Servers/Server_27050313/File/Departments%20&%20Services/Curriculum,%20Instruction,%20and%20Professional%20Learning/Curriculum%20Resource%20Teachers'%20Homepage/Curriculum%20Resource%20Teachers/Math%20Resources/TypesofProbs1st.pdf) (text says it is adapted from the 2011 progressions documents), states in prose:

- Result unknown is `A + B = □` or `C − B = □`.
- Change unknown (`A + □ = C`, `C − □ = A`) is “more algebraic and challenging” than result unknown.
- Start unknown (`□ + B = C`, `□ − B = A`) is difficult because the initial amount cannot be represented directly.
- The darkest cells of that table are the subtypes introduced in kindergarten. Grade 1 and grade 2 work with all subtypes. The unshaded, hardest subtypes are worked in grade 1 but need not be mastered until grade 2.

The shading itself did not survive text extraction, so this report does not reconstruct which cells were darkest. The difficulty order in the prose is clear.

[Saddle Up for 2nd Grade, 18 February 2025](https://saddleupfor2ndgrade.com/understanding-word-problem-types/), summarizing Carpenter and colleagues’ Cognitively Guided Instruction (1999), uses the same three join labels: result unknown, change unknown (second amount missing), and start unknown (first amount missing). The author, a teacher-seller, says to introduce easier types first, naming join and separate result unknown before compare problems. The University of Wisconsin Oshkosh CGI teacher guide linked from [The Recovering Traditionalist](https://therecoveringtraditionalist.com/math-problem-types/) did not load (`target_unreachable`), so the primary CGI manual was not read. The order above is established as a shared teaching progression across the standard’s examples, the progressions extract, and these teacher accounts. It is not a newly measured difficulty ranking.

Compare problems (“how many more”) are a further step in that table and are a poor fit for a single `X + Y = Z` card. Leave them out.

The same three slots transfer to multiplication because grade 3 uses the same idea: product unknown first in the Illustrative Mathematics account, then an unknown factor, with division defined as that unknown factor.

### A sitting is short and untimed; a drill is a clocked page

No fetched source states a number of missing-number problems per game session. What they do state:

- WWC [Recommendation 5](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/practiceguide/wwc_empg_summary_020714.pdf) says to plan math each day for young children and to tuck more math into routines. The evidence rating for that recommendation is minimal. The summary gives no minute count and no problem count. One game sitting is not the whole day’s math.
- [Holly Korbey, Edutopia, 20 October 2023](https://www.edutopia.org/article/how-decreased-practice-time-plays-into-historic-math-declines/), reports researchers saying that short, frequent retrieval beats longer, rarer practice. The article’s concrete school example is an extra 20-minute partner block (SpringMath) aimed at weak skills. It also describes one-minute fact sheets as the familiar drill. Twenty minutes is an intervention period, not a preschool game.
- [Jill Barshay, Hechinger Report, 30 May 2023](https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-do-math-drills-help-children-learn/), describes the contested object as a stopwatch worksheet lasting one to five minutes. She reports that the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics urges teachers to avoid timed tests, and that well-designed experiments have not settled whether those tests cause anxiety. [Jo Boaler’s youcubed essay](https://www.youcubed.org/evidence/fluency-without-fear/) (page `published_date` 15 March 2017; the essay text says updated 28 January 2015) claims math anxiety has been recorded at age 5 and that timed tests are a major cause. Barshay treats that causal claim as contested. The safe design consequence is the same either way: this game has no clock and no score.
- [Borgestig and colleagues, full text on PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867850/) (no publication date in the extracted metadata; references in the paper run through 2015), followed 10 children ages 1–15 with severe physical impairments who were new to gaze-based access. A target-selection test was capped at about 6 minutes because that was judged feasible across ages. Accuracy fell as more targets were presented inside a test (odds ratio 0.96 per additional target). Overall, 868 of 1,219 targets were hit (71 percent). Children with alternating strabismus stayed less accurate. This is not a sample of children who can also use a mouse. It is the only fetched measurement of gaze selections accumulating inside a few minutes.
- [4-C, 16 October 2020](https://www.4-c.org/social-emotional-skills-childhood-development/what-is-normal-behavior-in-toddlers/) (page modified 15 June 2023) states toddler attention spans of 5–8 minutes at 24–36 months and 8–10 minutes at 3–4 years. The page does not cite a study for those figures. Treat them as a claimed ceiling for a toddler activity, not as a measured result. The page stops at 3–4 years.

A drill, in these sources, is a timed sheet or a long practice block whose point is speed or volume. A sitting, for this game, is a handful of successful looks that ends when the child is done.

### Gaze answers are a few large cards, not a keypad

[Inclusive Technology’s classroom leaflet](https://www.inclusivetlc.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/InclusiveTLC-EyeGaze-Leaflet.pdf) (copyright 2015) and the longer [Complete Guide to Eye Gaze in the Classroom](https://s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/downloads.inclusive.co.uk/inclusive/inclusive-guide-to-eyegaze-in-the-classroom.pdf) are the same publisher’s practitioner guidance, not independent studies. Read together, they say:

- Selection is looking at a target for a dwell measured in fractions of a second. Blink or switch selection exists and, in their words, is rarely chosen.
- Skill builds from a single target, to dwell on 2 or more large targets, and only later to 4–8 or more targets, where they also introduce select-and-drag. A separate toy-control example uses 1, 2, 3, or 4 on-screen buttons.
- Early choice making should be errorless: any look is rewarded. Easy mode lets the child go at their own pace, “free from the fear of losing or running out of time.”
- First sessions should be frequent and short, with immediate feedback. Their mouse-emulation examples use a dwell of about 1 second and tell the adult to try shorter and longer.
- Targets should be enlargeable (larger cells, larger text, high contrast).

[Indigo’s 2020 handout](https://indigo.org.au/media/w1yj3oru/keys-to-developing-eye-gaze-skills.pdf), adapted from Inclusive’s switch roadmap, Linda Burkhart, and a Tobii learning-curve page, is a second practitioner source. It says to start dwell selection at about 0.8 seconds and adjust. Failure-free choosing is “one item from three or more” where every option is acceptable, on large targets that shrink only as skill grows. The next key, finding the correct item, is also “three or more,” with “an appropriate number” and a warning not to limit choices the child can already handle.

The product’s own dwells are about 650 ms on a play target and about 1300 ms on a menu. The 650 ms play dwell is a little shorter than Indigo’s 0.8 second start and Inclusive’s about 1 second example. That is acceptable only while cards stay few and large. If a card selects by accident, the dwell should move toward 0.8–1.0 seconds. It should not be replaced with a blink. The longer menu dwell matches the idea that leaving is a different, more deliberate act.

A 0–9 pad is ten targets. That is past Inclusive’s “2 or more large targets” stage and into the stage where they start adding drag, which this game forbids. It is also the small-key typing the design rules out for younger bands. Older bands do not need a second entry method. Four large cards can hold a correct value and three distractors at every age, including missing factors from 1 to 10.

## Recommendation

Use one generator. Each challenge is three slots and one operation, or, for Tiny Tots, a picture quantity with no operation symbols. Exactly one slot is blank. The child fills it by dwelling on a card. Difficulty is a change of range, operation, and which slot is blank. It is not a new game.

| Band | What is on screen | Numbers | Blank | Cards | Cap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tiny Tots, about 2–3 | Pictures only. No `+`, `−`, or `=` | 1–3 | The matching set, or the set that is one more | 2 | 4 |
| Little Pals, about 4–5 | Dots beside digits. `a + b = □`, then `a − b = □`, and only later `a + □ = c` | Whole numbers, totals 0–5. Sums and differences do not exceed 5 | Result first. Second addend only after result-unknown successes. Not the first slot | 3 | 6 |
| Shopkeeper, about 6–8 | Digits. `+` and `−` only. Optional last items may be a repeated equal addend (`2 + 2 + 2 = □`), still not `×` | Within 10 first, then within 20. No two-digit typing and no within-100 algorithm | Result, then either part, then the start. One position family per sitting until the easier ones are going well | 4 | 8 |
| Rush, about 9+ | `+`, `−`, `×`, and `÷`, still three terms | Addition and subtraction within 20. Multiplication and division are one-digit factors, so products are the facts through 10×10. Offer factors 1, 2, 5, and 10 before 3 and 4, and those before 6–9 | Same order: result, then a factor, then the other factor. Division is the unknown-factor form, not a new layout | 4 | 10 |

The caps are inferred. They are ceilings, not quotas. Nothing in the sources counted “eight equations.” They sit inside a few minutes of looking, under the about-6-minute gaze test Borgestig’s team thought feasible, under 4-C’s claimed 5–8 minutes for ages 2–3, and far under a 20-minute intervention block or a timed fact sheet. The child may stop earlier.

Distractors, inferred from Starr’s add-everything error and from the need to keep cards large:

- One neighbor of the correct number (off by one).
- When the blank is a part, one card that is the sum of the two visible numbers.
- The rest filled with other legal answers in range, not with negatives or numbers the band never uses.
- Tiny Tots foils are another visible quantity from 1–3, not a numeral keypad.

Do not put the total on the left (`5 = □ + 2`) until Shopkeeper, and not on the first challenges there. Kindergarten does record decompositions that way, but Starr’s equal-sign error is exactly that layout.

### Dwell steps

1. Show the pictures or the equation large and still. Looking at the problem does not select anything.
2. Show 2, 3, or 4 cards, spaced so a glance between them does not fill a ring. The ring grows only while the pointer or gaze stays on one card, for about 650 ms. Leaving the card resets the ring. Mouse and touch activate the same card. There is no blink.
3. A correct look writes that value into the blank and leaves it there. A brief, unscored confirmation, then the next challenge.
4. A wrong look leaves the pictures or the equation exactly as they were. No score, no buzzer, no cleared work, no “fail” stamp. The ring resets. The child chooses again. The card just rejected should ignore gaze until the child looks away, so a held look cannot immediately fail again. That cooldown is inferred; the sources ask for another try and for freedom from “losing,” but they do not specify a cooldown.
5. Done, back, and any other menu control take about 1300 ms. Play cards do not.
6. The sitting ends at the cap or when Done fills, whichever comes first. There is no end grade and no stored account. If a later challenge in the same sitting should be easier after a miss, that adjustment can live in memory only for this sitting.

### What not to build

- A 0–9 pad, at any age.
- Dragging, tracing, or a blank that must be written by hand.
- A clock, a streak, a high score, or a screen that says how many were wrong.
- Start-unknown, compare, two-step, parentheses, or remainder problems in this engine.
- Multiplication facts before Rush. Repeated addition is the only bridge, and it is optional at the end of Shopkeeper.
- Smaller cards in exchange for more choices. If four cards feel crowded, drop to three. Do not shrink them.

## Gaps

- No fetched study gave a problem count for a children’s math game. The caps above are inferred from attention claims, from one gaze-fatigue result in a disabled sample, and from the contrast between a short burst and a drill.
- The CGI manual and the Oshkosh teacher guide were not read. Blank order rests on the Common Core examples, a progressions extract, and teacher explanations of Carpenter’s types.
- Inclusive’s two PDFs are one publisher. Indigo cites that tradition plus Tobii. They agree on large targets, short dwells near one second, errorless practice before right-answer practice, and a small button count. They are not trials of this game.
- Borgestig’s accuracy drop is from 10 beginning gaze users with severe physical impairments. It is a warning about long strings of targets, not a norm for every player.
- 4-C’s minute ranges have no study attached on that page, and they stop at ages 3–4.
- Common Core is a United States grade target. A 4-year-old is not a kindergartner finishing “within 10,” and a 9-year-old is not automatically fluent with every fact through 10×10. The bands start easier than the end-of-year line on purpose.
- Whether timed tests cause anxiety is disputed between Boaler and the sources Barshay reviewed. The game still should not time or score, because that dispute is not a reason to add a clock, and because Inclusive’s own easy mode avoids the fear of running out of time.
