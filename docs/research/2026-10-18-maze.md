# Waypoint dwell mazes: look at the next junction, then let the corridor carry you

Play the maze as a short chain of large junctions, not as a corridor the eyes have to steer. Gaze, mouse, and touch all rest on the same stone. A legal dwell walks the token along the open floor to that stone. A look at a wall, a later junction, or the floor between stones does nothing. The maze never resets.

That is the only interaction that fits this game. There is no blink to confirm a look. Gaze cannot reliably trace a narrow path. Play dwell is already fixed near 650 ms, and menu dwell near 1300 ms. Targets have to stay large. The maze itself is generated, not a stack of drawn pictures.

What follows is the evidence. The level recipe after that is inferred from it. Pencil-maze grid charts and gaze-target studies do not measure the same thing, and they are not averaged into one number.

## Dwell the next junction

Continuous steering fails for two reasons that show up whenever gaze is asked to select something small. Spatial accuracy is worse than a mouse, so the estimated look falls off a narrow corridor. Unconscious fixations also fire actions the person did not mean, the Midas-touch problem. [Liao and colleagues (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/11/2/127) state both limits directly and answer them the same way: enlarge the selectable area, and require a dwell before the select commits. Their map task is the closest published analog to "look at a point on a path." Adults selected point features with a Tobii 4C. A 600 ms dwell beat both 200 ms and 1000 ms on efficiency. Accuracy kept rising to 1000 ms, but the gain from 600 to 1000 was smaller than the gain from 200 to 600, and they judged the longer dwell more likely to fatigue the eyes. For points, a hit radius of 1.5° of visual angle beat 1° and 2°. The larger radius was faster and the smaller one less reliable, but 2° started to hurt accuracy because neighboring hit areas overlap. A line is worse: the best polyline radius was only 0.7°, because a buffered line covers far more of the screen than a buffered point and collides with its neighbors sooner. A corridor should not be a hot strip.

The same paper is why a dwell point has to be a real step, not a bead every cell. They cite tracker spatial accuracy of about 0.5° for that class of device, and they note that commonly tested gaze buttons in earlier HCI work were about 3.3° to 7.7° across (radii roughly 1.6° to 3.8°). At their setup, eyes about 50 cm from a 14-inch 1920×1080 screen, 1° was about 0.87 cm, about 54 px. A 1.5° radius is a 3° disk, a bit over 2.5 cm on that screen. Foveal vision, as they cite it, is only the central 1.5–2°. A stone smaller than the error, or two stones whose buffers meet, will select the wrong step.

[Feit and colleagues (CHI 2017)](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/toward-everyday-gaze-input-accuracy-precision-eye-tracking-implications-design/) did not publish a single safe diameter in the abstract available here (the PDF was unreachable). What they do report, from 80 people, two trackers, and two lighting conditions, is that accuracy and precision can vary more than six-fold across people and across targets, and that screen region matters. A size that works in the middle of the screen is not a size that works in the corners. Design for the worse case, and keep stones inset from the edges.

Vendor specs are tighter than that everyday spread, and they are still not tiny. In a longitudinal study of 10 children (ages 1–15) with severe physical impairments who were new to gaze access, [Borgestig and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867850/) used a Tobii C12 quoted at 0.8° and a Tobii P10 quoted at 0.5°. The test still used the largest Compass Aim targets the software allowed, 100×100 px, and a 1 second dwell. Of 1,219 targets, 868 were hit (71%). Time on task improved after about 5 months. Accuracy improved only after 15–20 months. A missed look is ordinary, including for children who will get better. It is not a failed maze.

[Microsoft's mixed-reality dwell guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/mixed-reality/design/gaze-and-dwell-eyes) matches the 650 ms play dwell more closely than the 1 second clinical test. They suggest an onset of 150–250 ms in which nothing happens, so a glance is not a choice, then a further 650–850 ms of continuous feedback before the select completes. Feedback should be a mark that contracts toward a small end state, so the person can see when the dwell will finish. They also say novices tolerate a longer dwell and experts want a shorter one, and that a too-short dwell makes the world react to every look. The fixed 650 ms play dwell sits on Liao's preferred adult balance and inside Microsoft's finish window. It is shorter than the 1.5 s [Tobii Dynavox (2025)](https://download.mytobiidynavox.com/Resources/TDEyeGaze_Pathway_TDPilot_Instructions-And-Resources_en-US.pdf) suggests for general users who are choosing their own activities, and shorter than Borgestig's 1 s test. Do not "fix" that by shrinking targets. Make the stones simpler and larger so 650 ms is enough. Menu dwell near 1300 ms can stay on the slower, more accurate side of Liao's range.

Dwell-on-a-simple-mark is the case the literature says already works. A short design paper on gaze dwell ([arXiv:2204.08156](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.08156v1)) argues that a plain time threshold is appropriate for a simple target, and that Midas touches pile up when the target is something the person must read or inspect. Stones should be markers, not labels. [Kumar and colleagues (ETRA 2008)](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/gaze-input-accuracy/) make the complementary point: a focus point is a visual anchor the eye can sit on. Asking the eye to slide along a blank corridor gives it nothing to fixate.

Commercial gaze play that was actually readable here is selection, not steering. [SpecialEffect's Eye Gaze Games](https://www.specialeffect.org.uk/how-we-can-help/eye-gaze-games) are chess, checkers, sudoku, 4-in-a-row, and similar, with a setting for larger on-screen targets versus finer control, and the same games also take mouse, joystick, or switches. [Look to Learn](https://www.indigo.org.au/media/lgan0ilv/look-to-learn-activities.pdf), as catalogued by Indigo (2020), is grouped Sensory, Explore, Target, Choose, and Control. Early activities are "look at a region and something happens." Later ones are failure-free choosing. The [Look to Learn manual (January 2023 PDF)](https://hub.thinksmartbox.com/app/uploads/2022/08/Look-to-Learn-Manual_Jan-2023.pdf) does not describe a maze. [HelpKidzLearn](https://www.helpkidzlearn.com/resources/knowledge-base/assistive-technology-skills-development/eye-gaze-access/) describes the same ladder in one line: cause and effect, then sequences, then choice-making. Tobii Dynavox's 2025 pathway is explicit and not a staircase that must be mastered in order: Spark Interest, Large Targets, Smaller Targets, Choice Making, Expanding Use. Large targets come before choices. Early steps do not even require calibration. The first mazes should be one big next stone. Choices come after the child can hit that stone.

No fetched source describes a gaze maze whose rule is "look at the next junction and the piece walks the open corridor." That design is inferred. The sources do say what will fail if the junctions are packed: overlapping hit areas select the neighbor (Liao), a glance while planning the route becomes a move (Midas touch; Microsoft), and error grows toward the edges and across people (Feit). A straight run of cells, each its own dwell point, is the dense case. Collapse every degree-2 cell into one corridor. The dwell point is the next junction, the goal, or the single step at the end of the current open run.

## How to show the legal next step without a menu

Show the maze. Walls, a wide floor, a marked start, and a marked goal. [DoYouMaze (3 September 2026)](https://www.doyoumaze.com/blog/how-to-make-a-kids-maze) treats a labeled start and goal, an obviously open solution, and short obvious dead ends as the basics of a children's maze. The stones sit in the floor at the junctions you can reach in one step from where the token is. They are focus points, not a row of buttons beside the maze.

While the pointer rests on a legal stone, after the short onset, draw two things and nothing else: the contracting dwell mark on that stone (Microsoft's pattern), and a faint trail along the open cells from the token to that stone. The trail is the answer to "where will this look take me." It follows the corridor. It never cuts a corner. Stones that are not the next step stay unlit and do not accept a dwell. A dead-end branch is still a legal next step if the corridor to it is open; hiding it would turn the maze into a guided tour. The trail simply ends there. The way back is the next stone after you arrive.

One to three lit stones is the range that stays readable. More than that and the targets either shrink under Liao's overlap limit or cover the maze, which is the menu failure in disguise. [SpecialEffect](https://www.specialeffect.org.uk/how-we-can-help/eye-gaze-games) already offers "larger targets" as a change to the board, not as a separate list of moves. Do the same.

## What generation can actually control

A perfect maze is a spanning tree: every cell is reachable, and there is exactly one route between any two cells. [Wikipedia's maze-generation article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze_generation_algorithm) and [Jamis Buck's 2011 recap](https://weblog.jamisbuck.org/2011/2/7/maze-generation-algorithm-recap) agree on the texture of the usual algorithms:

- Recursive backtracker (depth-first) and Hunt-and-Kill make long winding passages and relatively few dead ends. Buck calls backtracker the algorithm with the fewest dead ends.
- Randomized Prim and randomized Kruskal make many short dead ends. More forks, shorter traps. Wikipedia notes that the usual Prim variant is easy toward the start and harder elsewhere, and that Kruskal's equal-weight spanning tree looks regular and fairly easy.
- Wilson's algorithm and Aldous–Broder sample spanning trees uniformly. They are unbiased and slow. A children's game does not need an unbiased sample.
- Sidewinder and the binary-tree algorithm are fast and strongly biased. Sidewinder leaves the whole top row open and has no upward dead ends, so it is trivial from the bottom. Binary tree has no dead ends in the direction of its bias, and two sides of the maze are open corridors. Buck calls those blemishes.
- Growing tree is the useful knob. Newest-cell selection behaves like backtracker. Random selection behaves like Prim. A mix sets passage length against fork count.

[Čarapina and colleagues (Algorithms, 2024)](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4893/17/9/404) measured this inside a maze game for children about five and up, who steered a character with simple commands. They are not studying gaze, but the structural counts match the classic account. Hunt-and-Kill and recursive backtracker kept the highest ratio of longest paths, which the authors reserve for later levels. Across sizes, those two also had the highest share of cells that continue straight (about 28–32%) and the highest share that turn (about 46–51%). Aldous–Broder and Wilson were the lowest on straight cells (about 18–20%) and the slowest to generate. Binary tree and Sidewinder were the ones they would use for early levels, because those mazes run straighter and turn less. That recommendation does not transfer whole. Their early levels are command sequences, where a long straight is easier to type. Sidewinder's open top row would also make a gaze maze solvable by always going one way. Use the straight-versus-fork finding, and generate with growing tree, not with Sidewinder.

Loops are a separate switch. The [maze-generator.net catalog](https://maze-generator.net/algorithms) describes a braid maze as a perfect maze whose dead ends are then opened until none remain. Several routes exist, which is kinder in a chase game and worse when the task is "find the way." This game already refuses a harsh fail. The kind version of a wrong turn is walking back, not adding a loop that hides whether the maze was solved. Keep one solution.

Wide corridors are not an algorithm feature. They are a render size. Generate a coarse grid, draw each cell large, and put one stone in the junction. If two legal stones would sit closer than the sum of their hit radii, reject the maze and generate another. Čarapina's app already regenerates a level that fails its own checks. The checks here are solution length, dead-end count, branch factor, and stone separation.

## Age, with the pencil charts kept in their place

Published maze "ages" found here are vendor pages about pencil tracing. They disagree, and none of them measured gaze.

- [The Maze Generator (8 July 2026)](https://www.themazegenerator.com/blog/maze-puzzles-for-kids-age-appropriate-difficulty-guide), a KDP-oriented vendor, says ages 3–4 should get a 5×5 to 6×6 orthogonal maze, almost a single route; ages 5–6 a 7×7 to 10×10 with a handful of dead ends; ages 6–8 a 10×10 to 15×15 with real branches; ages 8–10 a 15×15 to 20×20. Orthogonal only for the youngest. Thick walls, one maze on the page.
- [JigsawMake](https://jigsawmake.com/printable-maze-generator) (undated) puts ages 3–6 on a 10×10 "kids" preset with wide corridors and very few dead ends, and uses Prim when it wants maximum dead-end density for teens. That 10×10 is already the top of The Maze Generator's kindergarten band.
- [KDPEasy (21 May 2026)](https://www.kdpeasy.com/blog/create-maze-puzzle-books-kdp), a marketing playbook, says ages 4–6: path 0.5–0.75 inch, grid 8×8 to 12×12, 0–3 dead ends of 1–2 turns, solution 10–15 turns. Ages 7–10: path 0.3–0.4 inch, grid 15×15 to 20×20, 5–12 dead ends, solution 25–50 turns. Those path widths are finger and pencil widths, not gaze angles.
- [MazeDIY's age page](https://mazediy.com/printable-mazes-for-kids-by-age) (vendor; the page date 2024-01-01 looks like a placeholder) is qualitative: ages 3–4 are one wide path and few or no dead ends; age 5 can take a few choices; ages 6–7 can backtrack; ages 8–10 want many branches. Their [benefits page](https://mazediy.com/are-mazes-good-for-kids) says to start wide, short, and with few dead ends, allow wrong turns, and not correct them. They suggest a timer only as a race against the child's own previous time, and only in the older band.

[DoYouMaze](https://www.doyoumaze.com/blog/how-to-make-a-kids-maze) names the levers without ages: size, number of branches, length of dead ends, number of choices. For children, pathways stay wide and dead ends stay short and obvious.

Taken together, the only stable claim is directional. Younger children get a shorter route, wider floor, and almost no forks. Older children get a longer route and more, still mostly short, dead ends. Copying a 15×15 pencil grid as 225 gaze targets would violate the overlap result above. Use those charts as a ceiling on how many decisions a child of that age is asked to make, then collapse straights so only the decisions are targets.

## Level recipe

Generation is a seeded growing-tree perfect maze on a small orthogonal grid, then a compression step. Degree-2 cells become the interior of an edge. Dwell points are the current junction's neighbors in that graph, plus the goal when the goal is that neighbor. Tiny Tots does not need the spanning-tree step at all. Regenerate until the row in the table is met and every pair of simultaneously lit stones is separated by at least one stone-width of floor. No account, no network, no stored picture of the maze.

| Band | Underlying grid | Solution, in dwells | Dead ends | Lit stones at once | Timer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tiny Tots, about 2–3 | A single path on a 3×3, or 3–4 cells in a row. Not a full maze. | 2–4 | 0 | 1, the next stone only | None |
| Little Pals, about 4–5 | 4×4 to 5×5 | 4–7 | 1–2, each one cell, off to the side | 2 | None |
| Shopkeeper, about 6–8 | 6×6 to 8×8 | 8–14 | 3–6, each 1–3 cells | Up to 3 | None |
| Rush, about 9+ | 8×8 to 12×12 | 12–20 | 6–12, a few of them 3–5 cells | Up to 3 | None that can fail. A personal-best pace is optional and off by default |

Growing-tree mix, inferred: Tiny Tots is a random non-crossing path, not Prim. Little Pals is newest-cell (backtracker-like) plus one or two one-cell spurs, rejected if a spur is longer than a cell. Shopkeeper is about 70% newest-cell and 30% random. Rush is about half and half, so forks appear without a hairball of dead ends. Do not switch on Sidewinder or binary tree. Do not braid.

Stone size, inferred, and the same idea at every band: painted diameter at least about 3° (Liao's best point diameter) and, for Tiny Tots and Little Pals, toward 5–6°, inside the 3.3–7.7° button range Liao cites. At 50 cm, 1° is about 0.9 cm, so a 5° stone is roughly 4.5 cm. The stone fills about half the corridor so the floor and the walls stay visible. Hit padding may extend a little past the paint, and must not meet the next stone's padding. Younger bands get fewer stones and more floor, not a shorter dwell. Keep stones off the outer edge of the screen.

A dwell point is a junction or the goal: a degree other than 2 in the cell maze, or the end of the open run the token is on. It is large enough to look at. It is not a cell in the middle of a straight corridor, and it is not a button outside the maze.

Movement: the pointer is the mouse, the touch, or the gaze, and it has to rest on a lit stone for the play dwell, about 650 ms. The first 150–200 ms of that draw nothing. Then the stone's ring contracts and the corridor trail appears. If the pointer leaves, the ring cancels and the token does not move. When the dwell completes, the token walks the cells of that one open edge to the stone. The walk is animated along the floor. There is no jump. A stone with no open edge from the current junction cannot be lit and cannot be activated. Looking through a wall, or at a junction two forks away, does not move the token.

A wrong look does nothing. There is no buzzer that reads as failure, no lost life, and no return to the start. Walking into a dead end is a legal move. On arrival the stone behind the token lights, and the child dwells it to come back. The goal stays where it was.

## Risks

- 650 ms on a huge stone can still be a Midas touch if the child looks ahead to plan. Lighting only the next one to three stones, ignoring the corridor floor, and cancelling when the look slips are what keep planning from becoming a move. Do not light the whole future path.
- 650 ms can also be short for a young or poorly calibrated look. Borgestig's children missed about 29% of targets at 1 second, on 100 px targets, and Tobii Dynavox's general-user example is 1.5 s. A miss must look like "the ring did not finish," not like a lost game. If misses cluster, the stone is too small, too close to another, or too near the screen edge. Do not answer that by resetting the maze.
- Feit's six-fold spread means one child's comfortable stone is another child's miss, especially in the corners. One global size, biased large, is the right call in a game with no account and no per-child profile.
- Copying a pencil grid (10×10 for preschool, 15×15 for ages 6–8) as one dwell per cell will overlap hit areas and fire steps the child did not choose. Those numbers are decision budgets at most.
- Sidewinder, binary tree, and braid mazes each break a rule: a biased free corridor, or more than one solution. Growing tree on a perfect maze is enough.
- Calibration may be unavailable. Tobii Dynavox says the early pathway does not require it. Large single stones tolerate a bad calibration. Small grids do not.
- No study fetched here tested this waypoint rule with children. The recipe is a constraint fit, not an evaluated therapy protocol.
