# Child Sudoku: large cells, a symbol tray, and puzzles that only need singles

Play it as two looks, never as a pencil grid. Gaze, mouse, and touch do the same acts. Dwell an empty cell until it latches, then dwell one large symbol. The symbol writes into that cell. There is no blink, no drag, and no tiny candidate marks inside the square. Play dwell stays about 650 ms. Menu dwell stays about 1300 ms. A 9×9 of small cells is not a gaze target. Younger bands stay on a 4×4 of pictures with so many givens that every empty cell is forced.

That is the interaction this page can actually run. The age bands below are a mapping onto Tiny Tots, Little Pals, Shopkeeper, and Rush. Published kid-sudoku pages do not use those names, and they disagree by a year or two. The given counts are a generator recipe inferred from the "many givens, singles only" evidence, not a published table.

## What size of grid children are actually offered

A 4×4 uses digits or pictures 1–4, sixteen cells, and four 2×2 boxes. A 6×6 uses 1–6, thirty-six cells, and six 2×3 boxes. A 9×9 is the ordinary puzzle. Same three rules on every size: each symbol once in the row, once in the column, once in the box. [Sudoku a Day (30 March 2026)](https://sudokuaday.com/mini-sudoku) is the clearest statement of the small grids, and it says a 4×4 often takes a beginner 1–3 minutes and a 6×6 about 3–8 minutes.

The age bands on vendor pages line up in direction and not in detail.

- [Sudoku School](https://sudoku.school/sudoku-for-kids-guide/) starts at 4×4 pictures for about 4–6, 4×4 numbers for about 5–7, 6×6 for about 6–9, and easy 9×9 from about 8. Pictures are for children who know an image before they know a numeral. The same page says 5–10 focused minutes beats forcing a whole puzzle, and that one puzzle a day is enough of a habit. It also says to praise the process, not speed, and to stop when the child is frustrated.
- [Sudoku a Day](https://sudokuaday.com/mini-sudoku) puts 4×4 at about ages 5–8 and K–2 classrooms, and 6×6 at about 7–11. It tells a teacher to start with a single missing cell. The teaching move it names is the forced cell: if three of the four cells in a unit are filled, the last symbol is the one that is left.
- [Sudoku Online](https://www.sudokuonline.io/kids/numbers-4-4) sells 4×4 number puzzles as suitable for about ages 4–8, says each puzzle has one solution, and says guessing is not allowed. The on-screen play is already two steps: choose a cell, then a number on a pad.
- [Sudoku Bliss](https://sudokubliss.com/kids.aspx) says more givens make the puzzle easier, puts easy 4×4 at about ages 4–6, and hard 6×6 at upper elementary. Do not copy that page's grid facts. It says a 6×6 has 32 cells. It has 36.
- [My Homeschool Math](https://www.myhomeschoolmath.com/sudokukids.html) uses pictures, shapes, and colors for about ages 4–12, and its beginner boards are cut-and-glue. That motor pattern is the one this game cannot use.

No fetched page starts a real Sudoku at age 2. The sources that give an age start at about 4, and they start on pictures with almost everything filled in. Tiny Tots can still have a board. It should be a picture 4×4 that is already solved except for a few forced holes, not a first logic puzzle.

[Sudoku School](https://sudoku.school/sudoku-for-kids-guide/) also suggests pencil marks once a child is on 6×6 and "ready." That advice is for a pencil. It is the wrong drawing for gaze. See the risks.

## How long one sitting should be

Puzzle vendors and attention pages are not measuring the same thing. Treat both as ceilings, not as a required session.

[Sudoku School](https://sudoku.school/sudoku-for-kids-guide/) prefers 5–10 minutes and one puzzle. [Sudoku a Day](https://sudokuaday.com/mini-sudoku) says a 4×4 is often done in 1–3 minutes and a 6×6 in 3–8, which means one small board can fit inside that 5–10 minutes and a stack of them does not need to.

Attention figures found here are commercial rules of thumb, not a developmental study this search successfully read. The NAEYC chapter that search listed would not load. [Shichida Australia](https://www.shichida.com.au/blog/how-to-improve-attention-span-in-children-with-play-based-tips/) (page listed 4 May 2026), an early-learning provider, says a toddler often focuses for two to five minutes and a preschool child for about five to fifteen when the activity is interesting, and that the adult should not push the span longer. [Brain Balance](https://www.brainbalancecenters.com/blog/normal-attention-span-expectations-by-age), a clinic that sells attention programs, says "experts" use two to three minutes per year of age: about 4–6 minutes at age 2, 8–12 at age 4, 12–18 at age 6, 16–24 at age 8, 20–30 at age 10. Some of their cited researchers, they say, put the upper end at five minutes per year. Those two pages agree that age 2 is a few minutes and that age 4–5 can be longer if the child is in it. They do not agree on a number worth coding as a lockout.

So the sitting is one board for the younger bands, a second board only if the child asks, and no clock that can run out.

## Gaze: two large targets, not eighty-one small ones

Gaze selection misses for two reasons that show up in every dwell study read here. The estimated look is less accurate than a mouse, and a look that was only a glance will fire if the dwell is short. [Liao and colleagues (2022)](https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/11/2/127) name both, including the Midas-touch problem, and answer them by enlarging the target and requiring a dwell. Thirty-eight adults selected map features with a Tobii Eye Tracker 4C. A 600 ms dwell was more efficient than 200 ms and than 1000 ms. Accuracy was higher at 1000 ms, and they still preferred 600 ms because the longer dwell was more likely to fatigue the eyes. They cite tracker accuracy of about 0.5° for that class of device. On their 14-inch screen at about 50 cm, 1° was about 0.87 cm, about 54 px. They also note that gaze buttons in earlier HCI work were commonly about 3.3° to 7.7° across.

[Tobii's X2-60 specification sheet](https://www.spectratech.gr/Web/Tobii/pdf/X2-60.pdf) gives the conversion in millimeters: 1° of gaze accuracy is an average error of 11 mm on a screen at 65 cm. Under ideal conditions that tracker lists about 0.4° binocular accuracy. Ideal is not a child at a kitchen table. [Zhang and MacKenzie (2007)](https://www.yorku.ca/mack/hcii2007.html), an ISO 9241-9 pointing study, sat adults about 60 cm from a 19-inch screen and measured their tracker at 0.25°–1.0°, which was about 10–40 pixels in that setup. Their 750 ms dwell ("Eye Tracking Long") had lower throughput than a 500 ms dwell. Failures were timeouts from jitter and tracker error, not wrong clicks, because a dwell either finishes or it does not. A miss should look like a ring that did not finish.

[Microsoft's mixed-reality dwell note](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/mixed-reality/design/gaze-and-dwell-eyes) is the closest match to the timings this family already ships. They want 150–250 ms of onset in which nothing happens, so a glance is not a choice, then another 650–850 ms of continuous feedback before the select completes. The feedback they describe is a mark that contracts toward a known end, so the person can see when the dwell will finish. Too short, and the page reacts to every look. Too long, and the page feels stuck. Novices tolerate the longer end. The family's 650 ms play dwell is the short side of that finish window and sits on Liao's preferred adult balance. It is shorter than the about 1 second [Inclusive's classroom guide](https://s3.eu-west-1.amazonaws.com/downloads.inclusive.co.uk/inclusive/inclusive-guide-to-eyegaze-in-the-classroom.pdf) suggests trying for a single-click mouse dwell, with the instruction to experiment shorter and longer. Do not "fix" 650 ms by shrinking cells. Make the cells and the symbols large enough that 650 ms is enough. Menu actions stay near 1300 ms because leaving a puzzle is costly, which is the long side of Liao's range.

The same Inclusive guide says screens for this use are generally bigger than 10 inches, and it places the child about 50–90 cm from the tracker. Clicker, as they describe it, keeps the dwell running for as long as the look stays inside the cell. A hairline gap is not a target. [Smartbox Grid 3](https://hub.thinksmartbox.com/knowledgebase/eye-gaze-settings-in-grid-3/) (page dated 5 January 2022) is the same model: look at a cell for a dwell to activate it, with a highlight on the cell. Blink is an option there. This game does not use blink.

[SpecialEffect's Eye Gaze Games](https://gameaccess.info/eye-gaze-games-eye-gaze-setup-and-options/) already includes Sudoku, and the control choice they publish is the relevant one: "Classic" uses larger on-screen targets, "Precise" uses smaller ones. Their chess screenshots are the illustration. The board is not a field of tiny squares the eye has to hit. Dwell time is adjustable, and the countdown is a pie or a shrinking dot. That is the ring this family already draws. Their site also has leaderboards. This game does not.

A search result for a 2025 CHI paper claimed a minimum gaze target of about 3.28 × 3.78 cm for 95% of gaze points to land inside. The ACM page and the ResearchGate copy were both blocked, so that number is not used.

What the readable sources do support: a robust button is on the order of a few degrees, not one. At Tobii's 11 mm per degree at 65 cm, the 3.3° lower end Liao cites is about 36 mm. The family's 72 px floor is about 19 mm, about 1.7° at 65 cm, or about 2.2° at Liao's 50 cm. That floor is the minimum the other games already ship. It is small for a gaze digit. On a 4×4 the cells can be much larger than 72 px. On a 9×9 they often cannot. The digit tray has to stay large even when the grid cannot.

Two-step selection is how the tray stays large. [Sudoku Online](https://www.sudokuonline.io/kids/numbers-4-4) already selects a cell and then a pad. For gaze, the pad is the candidate list. It is not nine specks inside the cell.

## What "solvable" has to mean, and how to generate it

A proper puzzle has one solution. [Wikipedia's mathematics of Sudoku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_of_sudoku) defines a proper puzzle as a partial grid with a unique completion, and a minimal puzzle as a proper one from which no clue can be removed without creating another solution. Different minimal puzzles have different numbers of clues. The minimum for any proper 9×9 is 17. Gary McGuire, Bastian Tugemann, and Gilles Civario showed that by exhaustive computer search. Wikipedia dates the paper 2014. [MIT Technology Review (6 January 2012)](https://www.technologyreview.com/2012/01/06/188520/mathematicians-solve-minimum-sudoku-problem/) reported the same result: many 17-clue unique grids exist, and the search found no proper 16-clue puzzle. [SudokuWiki](https://www.sudokuwiki.org/17_clue_proof) agrees with the result and adds the caveat that this is a computer enumeration, not a short written proof, and that a Nature redraw of the sample grid was itself invalid. Seventeen is the floor under which a 9×9 cannot be proper. It is the wrong target for a child. A minimal puzzle is also the wrong target. Easy means many givens and an obvious next cell.

The same Wikipedia page gives the cheap necessary check for symbols: the givens of a proper 9×9 must include at least eight of the nine digits, because two missing digits can be swapped in any completion and both completions work. The page states a general version of that bound, but the formula did not survive extraction. The swap argument itself is the part to keep. Inferred for the smaller grids: a 4×4 must show at least three of the four symbols, and a 6×6 at least five of the six. That check is necessary. It is not a uniqueness proof.

[Norvig's essay](https://www.norvig.com/sudoku.html) is the practical solver. Two propagation rules finish an easy puzzle with no search: if a cell has one value left, remove it from its peers; if a unit has one place left for a value, put it there. Those are the naked single and the hidden single. His first easy example solved by those two rules alone. Hard puzzles do not. He also tried to generate puzzles by filling random cells until at least 17 were filled with at least eight distinct digits. He is explicit that this does not produce a unique solution. Many of those grids have several solutions, and a few have none. Puzzles in books, he says, have one.

Counting solutions is cheap on these sizes if the search stops at two. [Wikipedia's solving-algorithms page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudoku_solving_algorithms) says modeling Sudoku as exact cover and running Knuth's Algorithm X with dancing links is the usual fast way to find every solution, on the order of microseconds for a 9×9. [Ali Assaf's McGill note](https://www.cs.mcgill.ca/~aassaf9/python/algorithm_x.html) is a short exact-cover solver that is not tied to 9×9. A browser does not need dancing links for a 4×4 or a 6×6. Norvig's propagate-and-search, aborted at the second solution, is enough, including for one 9×9 at the start of a board. His 2006 Python times were about 0.01 seconds on easy 9×9s and about 0.04 seconds on hard ones.

Uniqueness is not the same thing as "a child can see the next step." [Logic Wiz (15 September 2026)](https://logic-wiz.com/blog/is-guessing-ever-required-in-a-sudoku/), writing about its own catalogue, separates the two: one solution, and a path of deductions that reaches it. On their boards, singles alone finished 99.9% of "beginner" and 91.1% of "easy," 38.1% of "medium," and 2.1% of "hard." That is a vendor counting its own puzzles. The shape of the result matches Norvig's easy-versus-hard split. A child band should be in the singles-only group. If the child has to guess, [Logic Wiz](https://logic-wiz.com/blog/is-guessing-ever-required-in-a-sudoku/) says one of three things happened: the puzzle has two solutions, a step was missed, or the technique is one the solver does not have yet. For this game the third case is a generator bug. Do not ship it.

[Cornell's 2009 teaching notes](https://pi.math.cornell.edu/~mec/Summer2009/meerkamp/Site/How_many_clues.html) are outdated on the 17-clue proof (they still call 16 "unlikely"), and they are still the right warning about almost-full grids: a 9×9 with four empty cells need not be unique, while a solvable 9×9 with three or fewer empty cells has at most one. "We only deleted a few" is not a check. Count.

[Andries Brouwer's uniqueness notes](https://homepages.cwi.nl/~aeb/games/sudoku/solving18.html) assume the puzzle is already proper. A technique that says "this pattern would make two solutions, so it cannot be" is only valid if the generator promised one solution. Children should not need that technique. The generator should.

## What to leave out

A countdown, a mistake total, and a percentile are the shame UI. [Sudoku Online](https://www.sudokuonline.io/kids/numbers-4-4) shows time used, mistakes detected, and "you're in the top 3%." That is the screen not to copy. [Sudoku School](https://sudoku.school/sudoku-for-kids-guide/) says the useful lesson is method, not speed. Inclusive's guide, describing Look to Learn, contrasts an easy mode with no fear of losing or of running out of time against a standard mode that adds competition. Young bands get the first.

Pencil marks inside a cell are a memory aid, and [Logic Wiz](https://logic-wiz.com/blog/is-guessing-ever-required-in-a-sudoku/) is right that they add no information. They are also nine marks in one square. That is denser than a gaze target. The tray is the mark: when a cell is latched, symbols that cannot go there are dim and dead.

An ambiguous puzzle has no right answer. [Logic Wiz](https://logic-wiz.com/blog/is-guessing-ever-required-in-a-sudoku/) says to stop, because no technique fixes two solutions. [Norvig](https://www.norvig.com/sudoku.html) shows how easy it is to emit those by filling cells at random.

Cut-and-glue boards, as on [My Homeschool Math](https://www.myhomeschoolmath.com/sudokukids.html), need a drag. Gaze cannot drag a small piece. Draw the symbols. Do not download a puzzle book.

## Level recipe

Generate on the page. One complete grid, then delete clues. Reject the board unless it has exactly one solution and a singles-only solver finishes it. Symbols are canvas drawings: four or six plain shapes for the young bands, digits after that. No external image, no account, no stored puzzle from a book.

| Band | Grid | Givens | What the child actually does | Sitting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tiny Tots, about 2–3 | 4×4 pictures, 2×2 boxes | 12 of 16 | Each empty cell is a naked single on the opening board. The tray offers only the symbol that fits. | One board. A few minutes. No second board unless they ask. No clock. |
| Little Pals, about 4–5 | 4×4 pictures, 2×2 boxes | 8–10 of 16 | A short chain of naked singles. Hidden singles are not required. Illegal tray symbols are dim and dead. | One board, inside about 5–10 minutes. A second only from "another." No clock. |
| Shopkeeper, about 6–8 | 6×6 digits, six 2×3 boxes | 24–28 of 36 | Naked singles and hidden singles only. Most rows are more than half filled. | One or two boards. No clock. |
| Rush, about 9+ | 6×6 digits, then a 9×9 only if the cells stay large | 6×6: 18–22 of 36. 9×9: at least 40 of 81, and only if singles finish it | Still singles only. No 17-clue board. If a 9×9 cell would be under 72 px, do not show that grid; pick the box first, then the cell inside an enlarged box. | One board, then "another" if they want it. No countdown. No rank. |

The given counts are the inferred floors and ceilings. The real test is the solver, not the count. A board that hits the count and still needs a guess is rejected. Empties are spread across the boxes, not piled in one. Givens must include at least three distinct pictures on a 4×4 and at least five digits on a 6×6, and at least eight digits on a 9×9. That last check is the one [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_of_sudoku) states. The smaller ones are the same swap argument applied one size down.

Generator, in order:

1. Fill a complete grid by backtracking. Shuffle symbols, and shuffle rows inside a band, so the next board is not the same picture.
2. Walk the cells in random order. Clear a cell only if two checks still pass: a search that stops at two solutions still finds exactly one, and a solver that may only place naked singles and hidden singles still finishes. Tiny Tots adds a third check: each remaining empty cell is a naked single before any other cell is filled.
3. Stop at the given floor in the table even if more cells could be cleared. Do not aim for a minimal puzzle. Do not use Norvig's random 17-fill. He showed it does not guarantee one solution.
4. If the screen cannot give every play target a short side of at least 72 px, and something closer to a few degrees for the tray, drop to the next smaller grid. A 9×9 that fails that test uses box, then cell, then symbol, each one a large dwell.

Dwell, the same for every band:

1. The pointer is the mouse, the touch, or the gaze. Entering a target starts the ring. The first 150–200 ms of a play dwell draw nothing, inside the 650 ms, so a glance does not start a commit. Microsoft's onset is that pause. If the pointer leaves, the ring clears and nothing happens.
2. An empty cell is a play target. At about 650 ms it latches. It does not fill yet. A given cell does not latch.
3. The tray is always on the page: one large button per symbol, drawn, not a row of specks. For Tiny Tots only the legal symbol accepts a dwell. For the older bands the illegal ones are visible, dim, and dead. Dwelling a live symbol for about 650 ms writes it into the latched cell.
4. Undo is a large play target. It removes the last symbol the child placed. It does not clear a given, and it is not a drag.
5. Hint is a large play target. It lights one cell that is a single right now, and it pulses the symbol on the tray. It does not write the symbol. The child still dwells that symbol.
6. Back, a new board, and "another" are menu targets at about 1300 ms. "Another" appears only after the board matches the stored solution. The cheer is not a score, not a time, and not a rank.
7. Mouse and touch hit the same cells and the same tray. There is no second interface.

A wrong look does nothing. There is no buzzer, no life lost, and no red tally. A finished board cheers and offers another. An unfinished board waits.

## Risks

- 650 ms on a large symbol can still be a Midas touch while the child is only looking at the tray to think. Latched-cell then symbol, a dead dim on illegal symbols, and cancelling when the look slips are what keep thinking from becoming a write. Do not fill a cell on the first look.
- 650 ms can also be short for a young or poorly calibrated look. Zhang and MacKenzie's misses were timeouts. Inclusive's classroom starting point is about a second. A miss must look like a ring that did not finish. If misses cluster, the target is too small, too close to the next one, or too near the screen edge. Do not answer that by adding a penalty.
- The 72 px floor is about 19 mm, under the few-degree buttons Liao cites as the usual experimental size. Digit keys should be larger than the floor whenever the screen allows. A 9×9 of 72 px cells is a bad gaze grid. Use 4×4 and 6×6, or box-then-cell.
- Vendor ages disagree, and none of them measured gaze or these four band names. Tiny Tots is younger than the sources' start. Twelve givens and a one-symbol tray is the honest version of a 4×4, not a harder puzzle with training wheels.
- A unique puzzle can still be unsolvable by a child. Singles-only is the second check. Seventeen clues is the uniqueness floor, not a difficulty.
- Norvig's random fill, a minimal puzzle, and "we only removed a few cells" each fail. Four empties on a 9×9 can still have two solutions. Count, and stop at two.
- Pencil marks, a countdown, a mistake counter, and a percentile are the dense and the shaming versions of this game. The tray and the cheer replace them.
- Attention numbers here are commercial rules of thumb. They justify a short sitting. They do not justify locking the page when a timer ends.
- No study fetched here tested this two-step Sudoku with children and an eye tracker. The recipe is a constraint fit, not an evaluated therapy protocol.
